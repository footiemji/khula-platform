# Vision: fast, disruptive, and not another Wonga

You've been explicit about this: Khula should feel as fast and simple as ordering an Uber, especially for the underserved salary-advance customer at a spaza shop — but it must never become "just technology" the way Wonga.com was. Wonga's collapse is a useful cautionary reference precisely because the technology worked; what failed was everything around it — affordability checks that didn't actually check, collections practices that trapped people in debt, and a business model that optimised for loan volume over borrower outcomes. Speed and responsible lending aren't in tension if the underwriting and collections are real. This document is about keeping them real as the product gets faster.

## What's already built to prevent a Wonga-style failure mode

- **Real affordability checks, not rubber stamps.** `server/lib/affordability.js` checks the *full* monthly cost — capital, interest, fees, and insurance together — against discretionary income, not just principal and interest. A loan that looks affordable on paper and isn't in practice is exactly the failure mode this exists to catch.
- **A human has to look at every loan before money moves.** Automated affordability + risk scoring can approve fast, but nothing disburses until a person has reviewed real documents and confirmed a credit check. This is slower than a fully automated payday lender by design — that's the trade-off being made deliberately.
- **Transparent, capped pricing.** Interest, fees, and insurance are all shown broken out, at or below NCA maximums, before signature — never a blended number that hides what's actually being charged.
- **A borrower can walk away for free.** The reconsideration window exists specifically so a fast "yes" doesn't become a trap if someone changes their mind.
- **Fraud checks that protect the borrower, not just the lender.** OTP phone verification and payout-account name-matching exist so a loan can't be taken out in someone's name and paid into someone else's pocket — a real hazard in exactly the underserved communities this product is meant to serve.

## Where this needs to go for the spaza-shop / assisted-application vision

The "Uber for salary advances at a spaza shop" model implies someone other than the borrower — a shop owner, an agent — is present at the moment of application, possibly on a shared device. That changes the trust model in ways worth thinking through now, before building it:

- **Agent-assisted origination is a different flow, not the same flow on a shared screen.** A shared-device application needs its own session handling (so one customer's data can't leak into the next person's session on the same device) and its own consent flow — the person at the counter needs to explicitly confirm *they* are the applicant, not the shop owner applying on their behalf.
- **DebiCheck's card-present mandate signing (Stitch supports this) fits naturally here** — a borrower could confirm their DebiCheck mandate on the spot via card tap rather than waiting for a bank app confirmation, which is the kind of speed this vision is going for without cutting the authentication corner.
- **The human KYC review can't be instant if it's genuinely human.** Getting to "Uber speed" for the underserved salary-advance use case likely means either (a) a dedicated fast-review queue with a real reviewer on shift during spaza-shop hours, or (b) layering in real automated identity verification (Smile ID) so the human review becomes a spot-check rather than the primary gate — but not skipping it, per the "not just technology" principle above.
- **Collections need to be forgiving, not aggressive.** A DebiCheck failure (insufficient funds) shouldn't trigger repeated same-day retries or escalating fees — that pattern is a large part of what made payday-style lending predatory. Whatever collections logic gets built should have a clear, humane retry/communication policy from day one, not bolted on after a complaint.
- **Track outcomes, not just volume.** Default rates, complaint rates, and repeat-borrowing patterns (is someone using Khula to survive a temporary gap, or getting stuck in a cycle?) are the metrics that keep "fast and disruptive" from drifting into "fast and extractive." Nothing in the codebase tracks this yet — worth building before scaling the spaza-shop channel specifically, since that's the highest-vulnerability customer segment.

## Suggested sequencing

1. Prove the current human-reviewed flow works well at small volume — real loans, real repayments, real data on default rates.
2. Add real automated KYC (Smile ID) and a real bureau integration, so the human review can shift from primary gate to spot-check without losing rigor.
3. Design the agent-assisted/shared-device flow as its own reviewed piece of work, not a quick adaptation of the current single-user chat flow. **Status: built.** `public/agent.html` is a dedicated form-based UI (not the chat widget — an agent typing on someone's behalf needs speed, not conversational pacing), with its own PIN-based login (`server/routes/agent.js`, 12-hour tokens so a shared device doesn't stay authenticated indefinitely), and a `resetForm()` function that is the single most important piece of code on that page — it clears every field between customers so nothing leaks across a shared-device session. OTP phone verification is still required in this flow, not relaxed — if anything it matters more here, since it's the check that stops an agent from enrolling someone using a number they don't control. Every application this flow creates is tagged with which agent and shop facilitated it (`application.agent`), visible in the admin console's new Agents tab alongside a rough per-agent activity count, which is the beginning of the outcome-tracking sequencing item below (you can already see if one agent's applications default at a different rate than another's, once there's repayment data to look at).
4. Build outcome tracking (defaults, complaints, repeat-borrowing) before scaling into the higher-vulnerability spaza-shop channel — you want to know if something's going wrong before it's happening at volume.
5. Only then optimise for speed at the point of sale — by that point, speed is layered on top of real underwriting rather than substituting for it.

## Answered: how many months of bank statements is normal?

Checked against several NCR-registered SA microlenders (Arcadia Finance, Atlas Finance, ClearLoans) — **3 months is the clear industry standard**, not excessive. It's what the platform already asks for (see `public/upload.html`), now worded explicitly rather than leaving it ambiguous whether one payslip would do. The reasoning lenders give matches the reasoning for asking at all: a single payslip shows income, three months of statements shows *behaviour* — spending patterns, other debit orders, whether the account is regularly overdrawn — which is closer to what the affordability check in this codebase is actually trying to assess.

## What Shoprite/Boxer Money Market actually do, and the one idea worth borrowing

Neither is a direct competitor in unsecured lending. Shoprite's Money Market is a low-cost transactional account — send/receive money, bill payments, airtime/electricity, insurance, and "shop on credit" at their own stores — not a cash-loan product. No evidence Boxer offers loans either; it's the same category of retail value-added services (bill pay, money transfers, insurance) under Pick n Pay.

The one structural idea genuinely worth borrowing: **the recipient of a Shoprite money transfer doesn't need a bank account.** They collect cash at any till using a reference code. That's the real answer to the unbanked-disbursement question below — not "give unbanked customers a loan the same way," but "disbursement doesn't strictly require the recipient to have a bank account if there's a cash-collection point in the loop."

## On serving customers without bank accounts

You flagged this as risky, and that instinct is correct — here's specifically why, so the risk is legible rather than just a feeling:

- **Disbursement without a bank account is the easier half.** The Shoprite pattern above — a cash-collection voucher redeemable at a retail partner — is a workable model if Khula ever partners with a retail chain or builds out its own agent network densely enough to serve as cash-out points.
- **Collection is the hard half, and it's hard for a structural reason, not a solvable-with-more-engineering reason.** DebiCheck and debit orders exist because they let a lender pull an agreed amount from a bank account on a schedule, with the bank enforcing the mandate. Without a bank account, there is no equivalent automated pull mechanism — repayment becomes "the borrower has to actively go somewhere and pay," which is exactly the kind of friction that turns a manageable short-term loan into a missed payment, a fee, and a spiral. This is a large part of *why* unbanked lending has historically either been very expensive (pricing in the collection risk) or relationship-based (informal community lending, group-liability microfinance models like the ones in `docs/COMPLIANCE.md`'s microfinance references) rather than something a fast digital product does well.
- **If this gets explored later**, the honest starting point is a pilot with a small cohort and a real collections partner (or a group-liability model, which is how traditional microfinance solved this before digital rails existed) — not a feature flag that quietly relaxes the bank-account requirement in the existing flow. The fraud and collections risk profile is different enough that it deserves its own design pass, not an extension of this one.

## The bigger platform surface (send money, receive money, merchant payments)

You're right that a WhatsApp-native, KYC'd, payments-adjacent user base can be sliced into a lot of products — P2P transfers, bill payments, merchant/business payment collection, and more. Worth naming explicitly so it's a deliberate choice rather than scope creep: **none of that is built here, and it shouldn't be built opportunistically alongside lending.** A P2P money-transfer or merchant-payments product has its own regulatory regime (likely a payment service provider license or a bank partnership, distinct from the NCR credit-provider registration this platform is built around), its own fraud surface, and its own compliance program. The lending product and its underwriting/KYC/collections discipline should stand on its own first. If Khula expands into payments later, it's a genuinely separate product built on the same trust and identity foundation (OTP-verified phone, verified bank account, KYC'd identity) — not a checkbox added to the loan application flow.
